# Employment & Labour -- Narrative Outline

## Headline Thesis
India has the workers. India has the growth. But the economy creates wealth without creating enough jobs -- and the people who need jobs most are the least equipped to get them.

## Arc Type
Contradiction (the economy grows AND unemployment festers; the workforce is enormous AND most of it is informal subsistence)

## Opening Hook
"57 crore Indians work. The economy grows at 7% a year. Unemployment is only 3-4%. Sounds like a success story -- until you ask what kind of work those 57 crore people actually do. 58% are self-employed. Most of that is subsistence farming or running a roadside stall. The headline numbers hide a deeper crisis."

The hook weaponizes the reader's expectation. Low unemployment + high growth = everything is fine, right? Wrong. The contradiction between macro success and micro reality is the engine of this story.

## Beats (ordered)

### Beat 1: The Participation Puzzle
- **Beat name**: 57 crore workers, but only half the country
- **Narrative function**: ARGUES that India's workforce is large in absolute terms but shockingly thin in participation. The Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR) -- around 56% -- is one of the lowest among major economies. For every 10 working-age Indians, only 5-6 are in the labour force. The rest are students, homemakers, or have given up looking. The male-female split is the sharpest cut: male LFPR ~78%, female LFPR ~32%.
- **Key data**: LFPR over time (total, male, female -- three lines diverging dramatically). The female line at 32% is the visual shock.
- **Emotional register**: Curiosity turning to concern
- **Transition to next**: "Those who are working -- what are they doing? The economy is transforming underneath people's feet."

### Beat 2: The Structural Shift
- **Beat name**: The great migration from farm to city
- **Narrative function**: ARGUES that India is in the middle of the most consequential economic transformation in its history. Agriculture employed 60% of Indians in 2000. Today it's under 45%. Services have surged past 30%. Industry remains stubbornly flat at ~25%. This is the textbook trajectory of development -- but India is doing it with a twist: workers are moving from farms to services, skipping the manufacturing phase that powered China, South Korea, and Japan.
- **Key data**: Three-line sectoral employment chart (agriculture falling, services rising, industry flat). The flat industry line is the structural problem.
- **Emotional register**: Tension (the transformation is real, but incomplete)
- **Transition to next**: "The economy transforms. But can the workers keep up? For the young, this isn't an academic question."

### Beat 3: The Youth Crisis
- **Beat name**: Diploma in hand, nowhere to go
- **Narrative function**: ARGUES that India's youth are the biggest losers in this mismatch. Youth unemployment (15-24) runs at nearly 4x the overall rate. India produces millions of graduates each year, but the economy doesn't create enough quality jobs to absorb them. This is where the demographic dividend from Census becomes a demographic disaster: the largest young population in history, and not enough work for them.
- **Key data**: Overall vs youth unemployment rate (two lines, youth dramatically higher). The gap between the lines is the story.
- **Emotional register**: Urgency / anger (an entire generation is being left behind)
- **Transition to next**: "If the young are struggling, women face an even steeper wall."

### Beat 4: The Gender Wall
- **Beat name**: 2 in 3 women are not in the workforce
- **Narrative function**: ARGUES that India's female LFPR is among the lowest in the world -- lower than Bangladesh, lower than Sri Lanka, lower than sub-Saharan Africa on average. Only about 1 in 3 working-age women participates in the labour force. The gap has been narrowing since 2017, but from an abysmally low base. The state-level bar chart (lowest 15 states) shows the geographic concentration of exclusion.
- **Key data**: State-level LFPR bar chart (lowest 15 states). The numbers themselves are the argument -- Bihar, UP, and Delhi at the bottom.
- **Emotional register**: Anger (this is wasted potential on a national scale)
- **Transition to next**: "And for those who do work -- men or women -- the nature of work itself is the problem."

### Beat 5: The Informality Trap
- **Beat name**: No contract, no safety net
- **Narrative function**: ARGUES that the quality of employment matters as much as the quantity. About 8 of every 10 Indian workers are informal -- no written contract, no provident fund, no health insurance, no paid leave. The sectoral breakdown shows why: agriculture and construction, which together employ over half the workforce, are almost entirely informal. "Self-employed" sounds empowering but mostly means subsistence -- a farmer on 2 acres, a street vendor, a gig worker. The 58% self-employment figure is the number that reframes the entire employment picture.
- **Key data**: Employment by sector bar chart (current shares). Self-employed at 58% is the hero number.
- **Emotional register**: Grim recognition (the "real economy" is not the one in the newspapers)
- **Transition to next**: "The informal economy has a geography. The divide between rural and urban India is a divide between two kinds of work."

### Beat 6: The State Divide
- **Beat name**: Same country, different economies
- **Narrative function**: ARGUES that unemployment varies enormously across states -- from ~1% in Gujarat/MP (which often masks massive underemployment in agriculture) to over 8% in Goa and 7% in Kerala (where higher unemployment paradoxically reflects higher aspirations and fewer people willing to take bad jobs). The headline unemployment rate is misleading: low rates in rural states mean people are working, but often for very little.
- **Key data**: State unemployment bar chart (highest 15). The annotation that low rural rates mask underemployment is critical.
- **Emotional register**: Resolution with complexity (no simple answer -- low unemployment isn't always good news)
- **Transition to next**: (Closing section -- no transition needed)

## Internal Consistency Rules
- **Denominator**: LFPR is always "% of working-age population (15+)". Unemployment rate is always "% of labour force." Self-employment is "% of total employed." Never mix these denominators.
- **Time period**: ILO/World Bank estimates for international time series (2000-2024). PLFS 2023-24 for latest domestic snapshots. Always label source.
- **Workforce figure**: "57 crore" throughout (matching hero display). This is total employed, not total labour force.
- **Self-employment**: Use "58%" consistently (from PLFS). "Self-employed" includes own-account workers and unpaid family workers. Define once, don't repeat the definition.
- **Youth unemployment ratio**: "Nearly 4x the overall rate" (not "4 times" -- it fluctuates). The ratio, not the absolute number, is the consistent framing.
- **Female LFPR**: "32%" (ILO estimate) or "37%" (PLFS) -- pick one source and stick with it across all beats. The page currently uses ILO.

## Closing
The reader walks away understanding that India's employment crisis is not about a shortage of workers or a shortage of growth. It's about a mismatch. The economy grows through capital-intensive services and technology, while the workforce is trained (or untrained) for agriculture and manual labour. The 57 crore headline hides a deeper truth: most Indian workers survive rather than thrive. The demographic dividend from Census section 2 becomes meaningful only if these workers have formal jobs, fair wages, and a path to upward mobility. Without that, it's just a big number.
