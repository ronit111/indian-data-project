# Environment -- Narrative Outline

## Headline Thesis
India breathes the world's dirtiest air, drinks from a water table that is falling every year, and yet is building solar capacity faster than any nation its size -- the question is whether the invisible crisis underground will overtake the visible progress above ground.

## Arc Type
Contradiction with escalating stakes (visible progress vs invisible collapse).

The current "contradictions" frame is the right instinct but too static -- it treats air, forests, energy, and water as parallel facts. The rewrite stacks them as a single argument with rising stakes: what you can see (smog) is bad, what you can measure (forests, energy) is mixed, and what you cannot see (groundwater) is catastrophic.

## Opening Hook
"Every breath a Delhi resident takes in winter contains 10 times the particle pollution the WHO says is safe. But the crisis you cannot see is worse."

Hero number: **53 ug/m3** PM2.5 (current). This lands hard because 5 ug/m3 is the WHO limit -- readers instantly feel the 10x gap.

## Beats (ordered)

### Beat 1: The air you breathe
- **Narrative function**: Establish the most visceral environmental failure -- air quality. This is the entry point because it is *felt* daily by hundreds of millions. It anchors the "visible crisis" half of the arc.
- **Key data**: National PM2.5 at ~53 ug/m3 (10x WHO limit). Indo-Gangetic Plain cities (Delhi, Patna, Lucknow) chronically above 150 AQI. State-level choropleth showing the north-south gradient. Top 20 most polluted cities dot strip.
- **Emotional register**: Alarm, visceral recognition ("I breathe this air").
- **Transition to next**: "But India's environmental story is not all deterioration. Look at the ground beneath the smog."

### Beat 2: The green shield
- **Narrative function**: Introduce complexity -- forests are neither a clear win nor a clear loss. The 25% national average masks a redistribution: northeast states gain while central/western India loses. This beat prevents the arc from being purely pessimistic and sets up the "mixed signals" middle.
- **Key data**: 25.17% forest cover vs 33% policy target. Diverging choropleth (Madhya Pradesh -372 km2, Mizoram +242 km2). Top 8 gainers and losers bar chart.
- **Emotional register**: Qualified hope, complexity.
- **Transition to next**: "If forests are the ground-level balance sheet, energy is the industrial one. And here, India's transformation is unmistakable."

### Beat 3: The energy race
- **Narrative function**: This is the beat where optimism peaks. Solar capacity 22x in a decade is a genuinely extraordinary number. But the beat must also deliver the sobering reality: coal has not shrunk, it has grown alongside renewables. India is adding clean on top of dirty, not replacing dirty with clean. Capacity does not equal generation.
- **Key data**: Solar from 3.7 GW to 82.8 GW (2015-2024). Stacked area chart showing fuel capacity mix -- solar/wind growing but coal still the largest block. Solar at ~18% of capacity but coal at ~70%+ of generation. 500 GW non-fossil target by 2030.
- **Emotional register**: Admiration turning to tension ("but wait").
- **Transition to next**: "All this energy comes at a cost. India ranks 3rd globally in total emissions, even though each Indian's share is tiny."

### Beat 4: The carbon arithmetic
- **Narrative function**: Introduce the fairness dimension. India's per capita emissions (1.9 tonnes) are a fraction of the US (14t) or China (8t), but total emissions are 3rd globally. The "right to develop" argument is real, and the per capita framing is the morally honest way to present it. But the line is rising.
- **Key data**: Per capita CO2 trend line. Peer comparison pills (India 1.9t, World 4.7t, China 8t, US 14t). Coal as % of electricity generation trend. GHG total (Mt).
- **Emotional register**: Moral complexity, fairness tension.
- **Transition to next**: "Air is visible. Carbon is measurable. But the most existential crisis is the one you cannot see -- it is underfoot."

### Beat 5: The invisible crisis (CLIMAX)
- **Narrative function**: This is the emotional and argumentative climax. Groundwater is the crisis that does not make headlines because you cannot photograph it. India is the world's largest groundwater user. Punjab extracts 165% of what recharges. NITI Aayog warns 21 cities may run dry by 2030. Unlike air pollution (which improves when you reduce emissions) or forests (which can be replanted), depleted aquifers take centuries to recharge. This is the irreversible crisis.
- **Key data**: Categorical choropleth of groundwater development stage (over-exploited / critical / semi-critical / safe). Punjab at 165% extraction-to-recharge. Reservoir storage by region. Stage summary pills showing how many states are in each category.
- **Emotional register**: Urgency, existential dread, call to attention.
- **Transition to closing**: Solutions exist (Rajasthan johads, Chennai rooftop harvesting, drip irrigation). The challenge is scaling what works before the water runs out.

## Internal Consistency Rules
- **Denominator**: All pollution figures in ug/m3 for PM2.5, AQI for city/state rankings. Never mix the two scales in the same comparison.
- **Time period**: Latest available year as default (2022-2023 depending on indicator). Trends shown for the most recent decade where data permits.
- **Energy framing**: Always distinguish between installed capacity (GW) and actual generation (%). The 43% renewables number is capacity, not generation. Coal dominates generation at 70%+. This distinction must be explicit wherever energy numbers appear.
- **Groundwater**: Extraction-to-recharge ratio (%) is the consistent metric. "Over-exploited" = above 100%.
- **Forest**: Change in km2 (not %) for state comparisons, to make the physical scale tangible.

## Closing
The reader walks away with a layered understanding: the air is toxic (visible), forests and energy are a mixed picture (measurable), but the water crisis underground is the one that could be irreversible (invisible). India's environmental future is not one story -- it is three stories happening at different speeds, with different levels of public attention, and radically different stakes. The crises you cannot see are the ones that matter most.
