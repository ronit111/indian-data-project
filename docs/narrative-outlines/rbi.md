# RBI — Narrative Outline

## Headline Thesis

Six people on the Monetary Policy Committee decide the price of money in India — and that single decision ripples into every home loan EMI, every fixed deposit return, every food price, and every rupee in your pocket.

## Arc Type

**Cause-chain** — One decision (repo rate) cascades through a chain of consequences: bank rates shift, lending changes, credit flows shift, the rupee moves, and prices respond. The story is a domino sequence, not a plumbing tour.

## Opening Hook

"The RBI cut the repo rate by 25 basis points last month. Your home loan EMI dropped by ₹X. Your FD return dropped by ₹Y. One committee, one number, two opposite effects on your life."

Lead with the most recent MPC decision and translate it into two concrete personal consequences — the EMI you pay (goes down, good) and the FD return you earn (also goes down, bad). This immediately frames the RBI's central dilemma: every rate decision has winners and losers. The current hero (repo rate + stance) is correct data but doesn't make the reader feel the trade-off.

## Beats (ordered)

### Beat 1 — "The price of money"
- **Narrative function**: Argue that the repo rate IS the most consequential single number in Indian finance. When RBI lends to banks at 6%, banks lend to you at 9-10%. When RBI cuts to 5%, your loan gets cheaper — but your savings earn less. Every 25 basis point change moves ₹X crore in aggregate EMI payments across the country. This isn't an abstract rate — it's the wholesale price of money that reprices everything downstream.
- **Key data**: Repo rate timeline (decade view). Annotated moments: the 2019-20 easing cycle (supporting growth), the COVID emergency cut to 4%, the 2022-23 tightening (fighting inflation), the recent cuts. CRR as secondary series (how much of your deposit is locked with RBI).
- **Emotional register**: curiosity (the rate is personal)
- **Transition**: "But the RBI doesn't set rates in a vacuum. There's a target it's trying to hit — and missing it has consequences."

### Beat 2 — "The target the RBI answers to"
- **Narrative function**: Argue that inflation targeting changed the RBI from an opaque institution to an accountable one. Since 2016, the RBI is legally required to keep CPI inflation between 2-6%, with 4% as the target. If it fails for three consecutive quarters, the Governor must write a letter to the government explaining why. This isn't soft guidance — it's a hard constraint that forces every rate decision through the lens of price stability. The chart should show CPI inflation dancing around the target band — inside it is calm, outside it is crisis.
- **Key data**: CPI inflation vs repo rate dual-line chart with the 2-6% target band shaded. Moments when inflation breached the band (2022 food price spike). The "letter clause" — when did inflation come close to triggering it?
- **Emotional register**: tension (will inflation stay in the band?)
- **Transition**: "When inflation is tamed and the rate is set, the next question is: how much money actually flows through the system? The RBI controls this tap."

### Beat 3 — "The money supply tap"
- **Narrative function**: Argue that the amount of money in the economy is a deliberate policy choice, not an accident. Broad money (M3) — cash, savings, deposits, everything — is controlled through CRR (locking deposits with RBI), SLR (forcing banks to buy government bonds), and open market operations (RBI buying/selling bonds to pump in or drain out money). When M3 grows faster than GDP, there's too much money chasing too few goods — that's inflationary. When it grows slower, the economy starves for credit. The RBI walks this line.
- **Key data**: M3 growth rate (year-over-year). M3 as % of GDP (structural deepening — India's has risen from ~70% to ~80%, meaning the formal financial system captures more of the economy). The current CRR and SLR rates as annotation.
- **Emotional register**: understanding (the mechanism clicks)
- **Transition**: "The money supply is the reservoir. Credit is where it flows — into home loans, business capital, and farm financing. The pace of credit growth tells you where the economy is heading."

### Beat 4 — "Where the money lands"
- **Narrative function**: Argue that credit growth is the real-economy consequence of monetary policy. When the repo rate drops, banks lend more. Private credit as % of GDP has risen from ~28% to ~50% over two decades — meaning more Indians have access to formal loans than ever. But this growth is uneven: retail loans (home, car, personal) have surged while agricultural credit has lagged. The composition of credit reveals who benefits from cheap money and who doesn't.
- **Key data**: Private credit as % of GDP (two-decade line). If available, the retail vs agriculture vs industry split.
- **Emotional register**: hope (credit access is expanding) then tension (unevenly)
- **Transition**: "Domestic credit depends on domestic policy. But India doesn't exist in a vacuum — the rupee's value against the dollar, and the reserves backing it, determine whether global shocks reach your wallet."

### Beat 5 — "The war chest"
- **Narrative function**: Argue that forex reserves are India's insurance policy against the world. At $600+ billion, they cover roughly 10 months of imports — meaning even if India earned nothing from exports for 10 months, it could still pay for oil, electronics, and essential imports. The reserves exist because of 1991: the last time India ran out of foreign currency, it had to pledge its gold to the Bank of England and accept IMF conditions. That humiliation built the institutional muscle that now stockpiles reserves as a shield. The reserves include foreign currency assets (US Treasuries, etc.), gold, and IMF holdings.
- **Key data**: Forex reserves area chart ($billion, two-decade view). Import cover months callout. 1991 context annotation (reserves were under $1 billion).
- **Emotional register**: reassurance (the shield is large) with historical urgency (it exists because of a crisis)
- **Transition**: "Reserves are the shield. The rupee itself — what it's worth, how stable it is — is the front line."

### Beat 6 — "The rupee's tightrope"
- **Narrative function**: Argue that the RBI doesn't fix the rupee but doesn't let it float freely either — it manages a "dirty float" where it intervenes to prevent sharp swings. A weaker rupee makes imports (oil, electronics) more expensive for everyone but makes Indian exports and IT services more competitive globally. A stronger rupee does the opposite. The RBI's job is to prevent panic — not to target a specific level. The steadily weakening long-term trend (from ~45/USD to ~85/USD over two decades) reflects India's higher inflation relative to the US, not mismanagement.
- **Key data**: INR/USD annual average line chart. Annotated moments: 2013 taper tantrum (sharp fall), 2020 COVID volatility, the steady post-2022 weakening. Latest rate callout.
- **Emotional register**: resolution (understanding why the rupee moves, not fearing it)
- **Transition**: None — this is the landing.

## Internal Consistency Rules

- **Single chain metaphor**: Rate decision -> bank rates -> credit flow -> money supply -> prices -> rupee. Every beat is a link in this chain. The reader should always know where they are in the sequence.
- **Time period default**: Latest MPC decision date for point-in-time references. Trends cover a decade (2014-15 to 2025-26) to capture the full inflation-targeting era.
- **Repo rate**: Same number everywhere — from hero section, through Beat 1 chart, through Beat 2 overlay. Source: RBI MPC press releases.
- **CPI inflation**: Same series in Beat 2 as in the Economy domain's inflation section. Source consistency with MOSPI/RBI.
- **Forex reserves**: Same figure in Beat 5 as in the Economy domain's external section. Source: RBI Handbook.
- **No jargon without immediate translation**: CRR = "how much of your deposit stays locked with RBI." SLR = "how much of deposits banks must park in government bonds." M3 = "total money in the economy." OMO = "RBI buying/selling bonds to pump in or drain money."

## Closing

The reader walks away understanding that the RBI is not a faceless bureaucracy — it's six people making one decision (the repo rate) that cascades into every financial interaction in the country. Your EMI, your FD return, the price of atta and petrol, the strength of the rupee — all trace back to that one committee room in Mumbai. The RBI's impossible job is to keep prices stable, keep growth alive, keep the rupee steady, and keep the financial system solvent — all at once. When it works, nobody notices. When it fails, everyone feels it.

## Cross-Domain Connections

- **Beat 1 (Repo Rate) -> Budget**: The government's borrowing competes with private borrowing for bank funds. When the deficit is large, the RBI faces pressure to keep rates low (to reduce government interest costs) even if inflation calls for higher rates. The Budget domain's deficit section is the fiscal pressure on monetary policy.
- **Beat 2 (Inflation) -> Economy**: The CPI inflation series is shared with the Economy domain. The Economy domain shows the outcome (prices rising/falling); the RBI domain shows the mechanism (rate decisions to control it).
- **Beat 4 (Credit) -> Employment**: Credit growth to small businesses and agriculture directly connects to employment generation. The Employment domain shows WHERE jobs are (or aren't); the RBI domain shows the financial conditions that enable or constrain them.
- **Beat 5 (Forex) -> Economy**: Forex reserves are the shield against the trade deficit shown in the Economy domain's external section. Same data, different narrative — Economy asks "why do we need reserves?", RBI shows "how are they built and deployed?"
- **Beat 6 (Exchange Rate) -> States**: A weaker rupee affects export-oriented states (Tamil Nadu, Gujarat) differently from import-dependent ones. The States domain's economic profiles connect to currency dynamics.
