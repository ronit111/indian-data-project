# Climate & Energy Transition -- Narrative Outline

## Thesis
India is simultaneously the world's 3rd largest emitter and the country with the lowest per capita carbon footprint among major economies. The energy transition is real and accelerating, but coal is not declining -- clean energy is being added *on top of* fossil fuels, not replacing them. The math of net-zero by 2070 requires both to be true at once.

## Domains Used (and why)
- **Environment**: Renewable capacity growth, CO2 per capita, coal dependence, forest cover. The physical evidence of both transition and inertia.
- **Economy**: GDP growth and the energy-intensity question. Growth requires energy -- can India decouple the two?
- **Budget**: Climate adaptation spending. How much is the government investing in the transition, relative to other priorities like defence and interest payments?

## Argument Arc

### Beat 1: The Energy Mix
Renewables (including hydro) now exceed 43% of installed capacity. Solar alone went from 3.7 GW to 82.8 GW in nine years. But capacity is not generation. Coal plants run at higher utilisation, so coal still dominates ~70%+ of actual electricity output. The transition looks further along than it is.
- Emotional register: Admiration turning to sobriety.

### Beat 2: The Carbon Arithmetic
India emits ~1.9 tonnes of CO2 per capita -- versus 14 (US) and 8 (China). The per capita moral argument is legitimate. But total emissions are 3rd globally and rising with industrialisation. The "right to develop" and "responsibility to the planet" are in direct tension.
- Emotional register: Moral complexity.

### Beat 3: Forest and Green Cover
Forest cover at 25.17% against a 33% target. The northeast holds the country's green lungs. Deforestation in central India is not offset by afforestation drives. Forests are a carbon sink -- losing them makes the emissions math worse.
- Emotional register: Quiet alarm.

## Why This Matters
The climate conversation in India is trapped between two truths: India deserves to develop, and the planet cannot afford more emissions. Only by looking at the energy data (Environment), the growth trajectory (Economy), and the budget priorities (Budget) together can a citizen understand the real tradeoff. India committed to net-zero by 2070 and 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030. The data shows the ambition is real but the gap between installed capacity and actual generation is the honest measure of how far there is to go.
