# Education to Employment Pipeline -- Narrative Outline

## Thesis
India enrols 248 million students -- more than most countries' total population -- but the pipeline from classroom to career leaks at every stage. Near-universal primary enrollment masks dropouts at secondary, a skills mismatch at graduation, and a labour market that absorbs most new workers into informality. Education without employability is an expensive detour.

## Domains Used (and why)
- **Education**: Enrollment scale, secondary GER trends, spending as % of GDP. The "input" side of the pipeline -- how many students, how far they go, how much India invests.
- **Employment**: Youth unemployment, LFPR, sectoral employment, self-employment rate. The "output" side -- what awaits graduates when they leave school.
- **Census**: Literacy, demographic structure. The baseline population context.
- **Budget**: Education allocation as share of total spending. The investment question.

## Argument Arc

### Beat 1: The Enrollment Story
248 million students. Primary enrollment is near-universal. But secondary GER, while rising, still loses millions to dropouts -- especially girls in rural areas, especially after class 8. The funnel narrows sharply.
- Emotional register: Scale awe, then concern at the narrowing.

### Beat 2: The Jobs Gap
Youth unemployment (15-29) is persistently 2-3x the national average. The painful paradox: graduates face higher unemployment than those who never finished school. Most employment growth is in agriculture and self-employment -- not the formal-sector jobs the education system implicitly promises.
- Emotional register: Broken promise.

### Beat 3: The Investment Question
India spends ~4.1% of GDP on education. NEP 2020 targets 6%. But the gap is not just about money -- it is about whether spending produces learning outcomes and employability. States that spend more do not always perform better; quality of spending matters as much as quantity.
- Emotional register: Systemic questioning.

## Why This Matters
Education data alone looks like a success story (rising enrollment, shrinking gender gaps). Employment data alone looks like a structural crisis (youth unemployment, informality). Only together do they reveal the broken pipeline: India is producing graduates the economy cannot absorb, and the economy is generating jobs the education system does not prepare students for. The mismatch is the story.
